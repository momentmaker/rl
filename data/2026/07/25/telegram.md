🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-07-25*

Three rabbit holes, one loose theme: what happens when you stop trusting the thing that looks right and go measure it.

*1. LLM-as-a-judge: trusting model-graded evals*
The argument moved off "is model grading good enough" onto "show me the kappa number." The ritual: humans apply a draft rubric, measure agreement, inspect disagreements, *freeze the rubric*, then let the judge grade against those labels — targeting Cohen's kappa above 0.6 on your slice, re-sampled monthly for drift. It holds only where the task is narrow: frozen rubric, reference answer, 0-5 or binary scale. The scary part is that failures are *systematically optimistic*, not noisy. The canonical disaster is a GPT-4 judge grading GPT-4 output, which looked fine until an expert labeled 50 production traces and judge-expert kappa came back at 0.31 — family bias plus under-penalized fluent hallucinations. Newer and worse: "preference leakage" (ICLR 2026), where the evaluator's taste leaks into the model being graded via synthetic training data whenever generator and judge share a family; the authors call it harder to detect than any known bias. So the shipping pattern isn't a judge, it's a hybrid harness — code checks and end-state assertions where determinism exists, a calibrated judge for the reasoning-shaped residue, humans on the flagged tail.

*2. Print-in-place compliant mechanisms and 3D-printed flexure hinges*
The first failure is orientation, not filament: bending stress must act *inside* a layer, never across the interlayer bond. Backwards, you delaminate in 5-10 cycles; correct, the same part lasts 10-20x longer. Annoyingly, half the guides say "print flat" and half say "align with Z" — one geometry from opposite reference frames, which is exactly how people get it wrong. Material is a cycle ladder, not a winner: nylon for real repeated-cycle springs, PETG for moderate duty, PLA Pro for stiff low-cycle prototypes, TPU for soft return. The ceiling is humbling: a good FDM living hinge runs ~500-1,000 cycles against 100,000+ for the same geometry injection-molded in polypropylene. Plain PLA is the floor at 10-20. The cheapest upgrade nobody does is annealing the hinge in boiling water and working it while hot. For print-in-place the killer isn't strength, it's *clearance*: extrusion width exceeds nozzle diameter and the melt bulges before it cools, so a modeled 0.2mm gap closes to ~0.05mm. Print a 15-minute tolerance gauge per filament instead of reprinting the part. What survives is thin-and-many, not thick-and-one: flexures held near 0.2mm, force built by multiplying elements — one recoil mechanism works "because there's 12 of them."

*3. Typology photography and documenting vernacular architecture*
Honest finding first: nothing matching "I shot every house on my street" went viral in the window, so the premise didn't survive contact. What's live is institutional — Fondazione MAST in Bologna is running "Bernd & Hilla Becher. History of a Method," 350+ prints, free, through September 27. The title is the story: museums are marketing a *reproducible procedure*, the thing an amateur can copy this weekend. The mechanic underneath is that a grid hands the viewer a sorting task a single photo can't. Hilla Becher compared typologies to biological systematics — family, species, subspecies — so twelve houses in a 3x4 grid stop being pictures and become a taxonomy you can't stop ranking. The engagement is participatory, not aesthetic, which is why it survives a phone screen. The Bechers' own "the question if this is a work of art or not is not very interesting for us" is the window's most-reposted line, and it works as a permission slip: it lets a hobbyist shooting bungalows say they're documenting, not making art. Its real mass audience never uses the word — r/AccidentalWesAnderson and r/UrbanHell run the same deadpan frontal grammar at five-figure upvote scale.
