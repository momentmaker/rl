🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-07-28*

Three rabbit holes, one shape: what everyone measures isn't what matters, and the real variable sits where you can't inspect it.

*1. Agent harness vs model: what actually moves the score*
The claim isn't new; what changed this month is that it got a measurement apparatus and a disclosure norm. *Stop Comparing LLM Agents Without Disclosing the Harness* runs the clean experiment — hold the model completely fixed, change only the harness, and Terminal-Bench 2 pass@1 moves 69.7% → 77.0%. Its proposal is a *Harness Card* alongside every reported number. The spreads are wide: LangChain went from outside the top 30 to rank 5 on Terminal-Bench 2.0 without touching the model (52.8% → 66.5%), and the Holistic Agent Leaderboard has seen single-model swings near 48pp. Put that beside the gap between the top three models on the board (91.9 / 88.0 / 87.4) and *harness variance is bigger than the model variance it's used to measure.* Sharpest new artifact: *Self-Harness* — the agent mines its own failures, proposes harness edits, and regression-tests them before keeping any (40.5% → 61.9% on one model). That third stage is the whole thing; a self-modifying harness without a regression gate is just drift. When people enumerate what's *in* a harness, context policy leads: "every token loaded into context degrades performance somewhere, so you load what's relevant and leave the rest on disk." And the practitioner win is a cost win, not a leaderboard win — one open-source harness took a bug from $6.83/207 turns to ~$1.70 by learning the repo once.

*2. Buy-it-for-life: how you'd even check*
The premise moved from "which brands are good" to "how would you verify that." Best line of the window: "What got swapped out — the steel gauge, the stitch count and the years of service life — sits in the parts you can't check from the aisle. That gap between what you can verify and what actually matters is the whole game." The most-shared workaround inverts the review: instead of rating products, track *ownership* of 200+ brands and grade each Approved / Watchlist / Former Great / Avoid — where "Watchlist" triggers on recent ownership change and conglomerate pressure, a *leading* indicator rather than complaints arriving years late. The last verifiable signal was the lifetime warranty, and it's been gutted quietly: L.L.Bean from no-questions-asked to a 1-year return, Eddie Bauer from a 1922 unconditional guarantee to 60 days, Patagonia the named holdout. The tell comes from the supply side — "a lifetime warranty answered only with replacements is a cost line with no ceiling," sustainable only via repair and spare parts. *So the signal isn't the word "lifetime." It's whether they sell you the part.* Meanwhile r/BuyItForLife stopped taking recommendations and started running experiments: a live week-by-week test of four tees from €20 to €150 (825 upvotes), against prior research finding no correlation between t-shirt price and lifespan.

*3. Whether the junior developer entry path still exists*
The damage is real and its shape is an age cohort, not a headcount: devs aged 22-25 are down 19% from their late-2022 peak while *every* cohort over 30 grew, 41-49yos up 14%. A downturn doesn't selectively spare the over-40s. The inflection dates to when assistants "stopped autocompleting lines and started completing tickets." But the 30-day delta is the reversal — Cloudflare targeting 1,111 interns (18x its 2024 cohort), Netflix formalizing new-grad hiring after 25 years senior-only, IBM tripling US entry-level. The "no juniors" consensus is cracking in public. The most interesting explanation is a model, not an op-ed: Enrique Ide shows entry-level automation can *reduce growth and welfare without reducing entry-level employment*, by reallocating novices away from the most productive experts — tacit knowledge is non-verifiable, so it only transfers by proximity. Flat junior headcount is no proof the pipeline is intact; what mattered was who the junior sat next to.
