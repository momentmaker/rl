🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-07-31*

Three rabbit holes, one shape: everyone measures the loud thing; the quiet one decides the outcome.

*1. What teams actually report after running spec-driven development with coding agents*
Almost nobody is reporting, and the star counts hide it. github/spec-kit sits at *124,743 stars* against a flat admission that "rigorous brownfield before-and-after data has not been published" and that peer-reviewed literature offers *no standardized metrics yet*. You are deciding on anecdote. The best field report isn't about specs at all: a healthcare data company with AI tools on every team looked great in seat count, then four engineers across three teams gave four different answers, and "none of the four could name the org standard, because their org did not have one." It fails at the *enforcement* layer before it fails on methodology. The waterfall objection also has a precise form — Kent Beck's, that SDD "encodes the bizarre assumption that you aren't going to learn anything during implementation" — so the real objection is to *freezing* a spec, not writing one. And the trap has no exit inside the workflow: an agent-authored spec reads like a senior architect wrote it, questioning it *raises* hallucination risk "because now it's reconciling your objection with a position it never truly held," and "nothing in the workflow guided me toward 'that's enough, go build.'"

*2. How hiring teams screen engineers now that the resume stopped carrying signal*
The most useful number is negative: only *14%* of hiring teams deployed AI-detection software. Instead 47% changed interview probing, 39% added in-person meetings, 31% added live practical tasks. The industry looked at authorship detection and declined — because the detectors fail in the direction that destroys an innocent candidate. One writer ran 11 detectors over 8 samples, four predating ChatGPT: her *2019* text scored 5% human on one tool and 100% on six others, while her genuinely AI-assisted fiction passed as *99-100% human on seven of eleven*. They don't miss, they *invert*. The experiment everyone is quietly copying came from an economics professor: take-home midterm averaged 96 with 40 perfect scores, the in-person final averaged *48.6*. Then he was told to file individual complaints per student — detection was cheap, *adjudication* is what didn't scale, which is why the corporate version skips accusation and just makes the in-person delta the score. Meanwhile *62% prohibit AI in interviews while the same leaders estimate over half of candidates use it anyway*. Root cause is volume, ~254 applications per job, so the counter-move is imposing cost rather than detecting intent. The quiet version: referrals are 7% of applications and 30-50% of hires. When the document stops carrying signal, the channel becomes the signal.

*3. What canvas editors and timeline UIs actually get built with*
The renderer question got a measured answer: rewriting a flame graph from SVG to Canvas took first render *448ms → 42ms*, and under 15x CPU throttle held 118.5fps with 1 dropped frame against SVG's 60.6fps with 58. The mechanism matters more than the multiplier — cost stops scaling with nodes the browser must style and lay out, and starts scaling with nodes you actually *draw*. The invoice is stated and unpaid: "a canvas is opaque to a screen reader… we don't fully have a solution for this yet." Then the surprise: the most widely deployed infinite-canvas SDK, shipping in Google, Shopify and Autodesk, is still *DOM-rendered* — culled shapes just stay in the DOM with `display: none`. So DOM isn't disqualified by node count, only by *visible* node count. What forces you off the DOM is *hit testing*, and the fix is lifted from game engines: broad-phase bounding boxes, then narrow-phase polygon math. The underscoped subsystem is *undo* — about a week to specify upfront versus *3-5 weeks to repair after launch*. Best tell: a company with 55K stars monetizes the *timeline widget*, not the renderer.
