🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-08-22*

Three cases where the headline number turns out not to be the thing doing the work.

*1. AI companies destroying the books they scan*
A bookseller who received an anonymous order for ~1,000 obscure titles hid an *AirTag* in one volume. 404 Media followed it to *Amazon warehouse LAS8 in Las Vegas*, where employees say their only job is taking in bulk deliveries and *slicing the covers off* so pages scan faster, leaving every volume unusable. Non-destructive scanning exists — V-shaped cradles, overhead planetary rigs — and is simply *slower and more expensive*. Nobody chooses destruction; they choose throughput. The reframing that matters: on *21 August* a dozen-plus civil society groups asked the FTC to investigate on *antitrust, not copyright*. They explicitly do *not* ask to restrict model training — they ask whether destroying the last copies is an unfair method of competition that "starves the market" of source material rivals would need. Copyright asks who owes whom for a copy; this asks whether the original still exists for anyone else. Court filings reported in January say Anthropic spent millions buying books and removing spines to scan into Claude; its earlier settlement ran *$1.5B, ~$200 per title*. The counter-offer — volunteers scanning rare books — runs against a warehouse doing this full time.

*2. Japan's overtourism crackdown, arriving as visitors leave*
First half of 2026: *21.1M visitors, down 2.0%* — the first decline in five years. Yet 2026 is exactly when the fees land. Composition hides more than the total: *Chinese visitors are down 56.4%* as Beijing urges citizens to avoid Japan, while other markets absorb *72%* of the shortfall and Q1 still set a record above *10.68M*. The inversion worth keeping: Mount Fuji now needs a *¥4,000 permit* booked online, and the Yoshida Trail has a *4,000-hiker daily cap* — which was *not reached on a single day in 2024 or 2025*. A limit that never binds isn't a limit; it's a communications device. The money and the mandatory booking are what change behaviour. Kyoto is blunter: from *1 March 2026* its lodging tax rises up to *tenfold*, to ¥10,000 per person per night on rooms of ¥100,000+, with officials saying outright that tourists must bear the cost. Departure tax triples *¥1,000 → ¥3,000* in July, funding a target of *100 regions* by 2030, up from 47. Meanwhile r/japan's biggest threads are about capping *foreign residents*, not tourists. Totals falling while the same sites stay jammed means the problem was always *distribution, not volume*.

*3. An autism spectrum "too broad to be useful"*
Gothenburg and Karolinska went through Swedish records covering *3M+ people* born 1988-2016 and the *81,286* diagnosed with autism 2001-2020. Diagnoses rose *~800%*. The share who also had an intellectual disability fell from *55.8% to 6.7%*; diagnoses without it rose *~1800%*. It's a total-population registry, so "you're just measuring who showed up" barely applies. The same numbers support opposite conclusions, and both camps are serious. Dame *Uta Frith*, whose 1960s-70s work founded the field, argues the umbrella is *"close to collapse"* and has *"lost all meaning"* as a category spanning people needing round-the-clock care and people living independently. Her fix isn't a narrower line but *precise subgroups*. Read the same collapse the other way and it says a group always present was finally being found. The engagement gap is the tell: the study finding the post-pandemic rise was driven overwhelmingly by *girls and women previously unrecognised* took *18,924 upvotes* on r/science, roughly *twice* the broadening study's 9,570. The two aren't in conflict: a sex-skewed correction produces exactly that profile. Underneath, it's a measurement problem in medical clothes: *counting cases has a correct answer; deciding what counts as a case does not*, and no data resolves it, because the disagreement is about what the category is for.
