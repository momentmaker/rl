🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-08-16*

Three confident numbers, three reasons they aren't what you think.

*1. How stale light pollution maps are, and whether Bortle matches what you see*
The mismatch is structural, not chronological. djlorenz's atlas — the model behind most maps — says it plainly: the maps simulate brightness *at zenith*, straight up, while Bortle is a subjective judgment across the *whole sky*, horizon to zenith. The two "are too different to equate." So the map cannot see what actually ruins the photo: "the massive dome of light pollution on the horizon from a city 50 miles away." A motorway-edge site and a 1,600 m alpine site both print *Bortle 4*. On staleness: the latest *completed* satellite layer is 2025, and anything labelled 2026 is a provisional estimate — a year behind, not a decade. The real error is bigger. Globe at Night's 51,000+ naked-eye observations put sky brightening at *9.6%/yr* globally against satellite-measured emissions growth of *2.2%/yr* — roughly *6x*. VIIRS is blind to the blue LEDs emit, and weights upward light while *horizontal* light builds skyglow. The field has quietly moved to SQM in mag/arcsec². One Bortle class spans a *full magnitude* (Bortle 5 = SQM 20.3-21.3), which is exactly the information the label throws away.

*2. What it costs and what breaks running your own staff of always-on agents*
The VPS is the rounding error. Hosting runs *$4/mo* for a 512 MiB droplet, about *$40* for a backed-up, monitored, isolated host ($8-10 on Hetzner, $60-80 on AWS). A single low-volume assisted agent burns *$150-600/mo in tokens*; semi-autonomous ones $1,200-5,500. Self-hosting buys control, not savings. The expensive failure isn't a crash, it's a *conversation*: four agents in a LangChain pipeline ran eleven days and produced a *$47,000* bill, an Analyzer and Verifier ping-ponging with no budget cap. Every control was already in place — a Helicone dashboard, Slack alerts at 50/80/95%, a provider-level OpenAI cap. The line worth keeping: dashboards, alerts and provider caps are *observability, not enforcement*. Anthropic's own 28 April post-mortem is the same class of bug — hook chain recursion with no timeout or depth limit. Uber burned its full-year 2026 AI coding budget in four months; one enterprise hit *$500M* in a single month. The month's shipped answer is blast-radius reduction, not smarter loops: Docker Sandboxes (*693 pts* on HN) giving each agent its own microVM, daemon and filesystem.

*3. What solar shade reports miss that an on-site survey catches*
The surprise is that the shade math already passed. NREL tested HelioScope's remote solar access values against field SunEye readings at *85 roof points* across LA, Denver and Camden — statistically equivalent, error *-7.0% to +4.3%*. Six states' rebate authorities now accept remote analysis instead of on-site measurement. But accuracy is a *tier*, not a number: ~±2-3% where LiDAR covers you, ±5-8% on satellite only — and the report doesn't say which you got. What the site visit catches is everything that *isn't* shade: vents, pipes, skylights, roof age, panel labels, wiring paths, attic access, structural framing. A forgotten vent costs a panel on install day. The one shading factor no imagery models is *future tree growth* — a satellite pass is a photo of a tree on a date, against a 25-year production claim. The stated rule: field-verify when the roof is complex or the system exceeds 25 kW, because remote speeds up sales while field data protects engineering. And it's contested because shading is the *named exclusion* in production guarantees — the shade report caps the remedy it helped sell. The litigated failure isn't the model, it's stale imagery under a valid model: satellite showing no shade while street photos the same week showed trees. Against a reported ~12% under-model pattern, "it's the shading" is the cheapest explanation and often wrong — one 18-panel system ran at 6.8 kWh/day for 2.5 months because two thirds of the panels were miswired.
