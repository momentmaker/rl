🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-08-17*

Three things people are confident about, and what's actually underneath.

*1. Why every cause of hallucination produces the same four shapes*
Heinrich Klüver sorted every geometric hallucination into four *form constants* — lattices/honeycombs, cobwebs, tunnels/funnels, and spirals — and the claim that survived a century is that the *same four* recur across psychedelics, migraine aura, sensory deprivation, near-death and strobe light. They're universal for a geometric reason: the retina-to-V1 map is roughly a *complex logarithm*, so circles and spirals in your visual field become straight parallel *stripes* on the cortical sheet. The simplest thing a destabilized neural sheet can do is form stripes (a *Turing instability*) — run the map backwards and those stripes become tunnels and spirals in what you "see." Ermentrout & Cowan (1979) got the rotationally symmetric constants this way; the 2001 Bressloff–Cowan model added the cortex's orientation-column wiring and recovered the lattices too, so all four fall out of *one equation*. The causes differ only in *how* they destabilize the cortex — psychedelics remove inhibition, migraine aura is a traveling wave, near-death is peripheral-first hypoxia, Ganzfeld is input starvation. Same output, four routes in: form constants are the *eigenmodes of the visual cortex*, not you.

*2. What self-hosters actually run to get an alert to their phone*
The center of gravity is *ntfy* — a ~20MB HTTP-native notification server that nearly every homelab how-to routes alerts through (its "Push to Mobile" post hit 125 points on Hacker News this month). The one real fork is ntfy against *Gotify*: Gotify for granular access control and plugins, ntfy for publish-from-curl minimalism with per-topic ACLs on a 512 MB host. *Uptime Kuma* (90K stars) generates the alerts; *Apprise* is the universal fan-out — one config speaking Discord, Telegram, ntfy and the rest. To escape Google/Apple push you run ntfy as a *UnifiedPush* distributor on de-Googled Android — but the honest caveat is *iOS*, where Apple background delivery is the one piece a self-hosted stack can't fully own. Why build any of this? *"The container is up" is a lie.* Docker reports that a process exists, not that the service still answers — one self-hoster's camera sat dark for *sixteen days* with no error logged. Add a dead-man's-switch (Healthchecks.io) so a *missing* ping — a skipped backup, a silent cron — still pages you.

*3. Whether AI-text detectors catch anything, and whether humanizers beat them*
The most-cited number in the debate is a *false-positive* rate, not a catch rate: a Stanford study found seven GPT detectors falsely flagged *61.3%* of non-native (TOEFL) essays as AI while scoring native writers almost perfectly. That asymmetry — human ESL writing reads as "AI" — is the load-bearing fact under the 2026 backlash. Institutions are quietly *walking away*: the FT reports universities dropping detectors, Inside Higher Ed runs "AI Detectors Are Out," and *Turnitin itself* is pivoting from text-matching to keystroke surveillance, conceding its own detector "often fails." The reductio is a Yale lawsuit where GPTZero rated a dean's and a former president's decades-old writing at *"100% AI."* Humanizers are a crowded market, but even their own review blogs admit the bypass is *inconsistent* — a multi-pass pipeline, not one button — and a vocal minority calls it a cop-out. Watermarking is the month's live fight: Anthropic shipped C2PA marks, and the verdict is *"trivially removable,"* the nuance most miss being that a clean scan proves "no vendor watermark," not "a human wrote this." The tells have hardened into folklore (the em-dash, "delve," the "it's not just X, it's Y" tic). And the most-shared *detection win* wasn't a detector at all: a professor hid white text in an assignment telling any chatbot to insert "Madagascar," catching 32 students who paste-farmed it. Detection is moving from statistics to *traps*.
