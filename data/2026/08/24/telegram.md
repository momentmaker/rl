🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-08-24*

Three cases where the mechanism exists and one implementation detail decides whether it does anything.

*1. US customs bill-of-lading data and supplier discovery*
Every ocean shipment into the US generates a manifest record, and vendors like ImportGenius and Panjiva resell that same government pipe — CBP's ACE — so you can read a competitor's suppliers. The counter-move is not a lawsuit, it is a form. Under *19 CFR 103.31(d)* an importer can request confidential treatment of its own and its shippers' names and addresses across *22 manifest data elements*. No fee, processed in *under 24 hours*, every US port, *good for two years*. The catch is the whole story: it is *exact string matching* against what the carrier typed into ACE, so a company filing under four spellings must list all four or it leaks through the one it forgot. On the reading side: absence is *not* proof a company does not import, and when a forwarder consolidates cargo the *master bill names the forwarder* while *the house bill underneath carries the real consignee* — read only the master level and you build a prospect list of logistics companies. Declared values are customs filings, not margin data. Notably the 30-day discussion layer is silent, which reads as settled tradecraft rather than lost interest.

*2. AI weather models in operational forecasting*
*ECMWF AIFS* has run alongside the physics-based IFS since February 2025. On *22 August* hurricane modeler Andy Hazelton noted GFS and ECMWF were both aggressive on a Main Development Region wave while AIFS and DeepMind held off, then said the quiet part: "I think I'd defer to the AI models a little more after seeing their performance the last year or so." That is three ML models used as a consensus block *against* a physics model, in public, mid-season. Every headline gain has a regression printed next to it in the same paper: AIFS-CRPS is *5-20% better* than the IFS ensemble on upper-air fields and *degrades above 100 hPa*, with about *30% 2m temperature RMSE degradation in the Arctic*. The failure mode practitioners actually name is not error, it is uselessness — one killed an AI ensemble hurricane probability in a line: "about climatology or 'going rate'... not really adding value." The models also *regress toward normal in the tails*, under-forecasting record heat, with error scaling by how badly the record breaks. HN corrected the "extra day of warning" framing from inside the field: it is a *narrower cone*, not extra prep time. And they all train on ERA5 — which is physics output. The biggest weather thread of the month was not about AI at all. It was about weather balloons and buoys being cut.

*3. California's DELETE Act and whether removals stick*
On *1 August 2026* the law flipped from theory to obligation: every registered data broker must pull the DROP list and delete, then *do it again every 45 days*. *600+* brokers in scope, *300,000+* Californians enrolled, *$200 per consumer per day* for violations. The design choice worth stealing: DROP is a *standing suppression list*, not a one-shot delete. Re-acquired data gets deleted on the next pass, and brokers may not sell newly collected data about anyone with an active request. That is the answer to repopulation the DIY world never had: manual opt-outs are point-in-time, which is why records came back. The benchmark is brutal: Consumer Reports tracked *332 data instances* for 28 people across seven paid removal services and only *117, or 35%*, came down within four months, while *doing it yourself cleared 70% in one week*. Some removal services even had financial partnerships with the people-search sites they removed from. First enforcement was about paperwork rather than deletion: *$116,490* against LocateSmarter, whose opt-out form demanded Social Security digits, and *$52,400* against Cybba for never registering. And removal is not invisibility: the same guides confirming a 72-hour takedown note records persisting in sponsored results.
