🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-08-19*

Three cases where the safeguard everyone points at covers the cheap part of the problem.

*1. Bankruptcy data auctions and what happens to customer data*
Google won the Spirit Aviation bankruptcy auction at *$10 million* — and the thing everyone called a customer-data sale explicitly *excludes the customers*. Carved out: 97.5M passengers, 52.4M loyalty members, 740,000 cardholders. Sold: roughly *100 million emails, 500 million Teams records, 17 million OneDrive files, 30 million lines of code and 175,000+ employee files*. That inversion is the whole legal story: BAPCPA's consumer privacy ombudsman and §363(b)(1) reach PII *collected from individuals*, so corporate communications stay company property — "the company owns it free & clear." What actually stalled the deal wasn't a privacy mechanism but a *flight attendants' union objection*, moving the hearing from 19 Aug to 9 Sept. One fact sits in exactly one source: Google *designated and paid for its own deidentifier*, and the scrub must preserve referential integrity across the archive — "not what an independent audit sounds like." Precedent says it goes through. Toysmart, RadioShack and 23andMe all ended in conditions on the buyer, never a blocked sale; 23andMe's data moved for ~$305M and redress arrived later as $18M from 42 AGs plus a $46.75M class settlement. A privacy policy is a contract with a corporate entity, not a property right that survives it.

*2. Coding agent status lines and terminal prompt customization*
The one fact everything follows from: a status line isn't a theme, it's *a shell command re-executed on every render*, handed a fresh JSON payload of cost, context, duration and token counts. What people put in it is the meter, not the model — context percentage, session spend, and "the rate limits Anthropic doesn't surface, including per-model weekly caps." Adoption consolidated on *ccstatusline* (12,472 stars), already cross-agent across five CLIs. Here's what the agent side hasn't read: the prompt world priced this exact cost years ago. Powerlevel10k stopped shelling out entirely — *gitstatusd* is a persistent C++ daemon returning git state in *under 10ms regardless of repo size*, against a stated target of staying well under *50ms*, roughly where a human starts noticing. The 2026 agent-side guidance is still "keep it local, cache when possible." oh-my-posh's maintainer spent August defending native git status "10x faster on Windows, 4x on Unix (measured, not estimated)" against a Starship switch post. And the real failure mode isn't slowness, it's *silence*: one wrong key in `starship.toml` and the segment never renders — "no error, no warning, it just quietly never fired."

*3. Cloud vendor lock-in and the real cost of switching*
The date that decides everything is *12 January 2027*, when Art. 29 of the EU Data Act removes switching and egress charges outright. Brussels is regulating the same problem twice: the Commission's late-June preliminary position would designate AWS and Azure as DMA gatekeepers on *lock-in and switching-cost* grounds rather than market share — neither reaches the ~40% dominance threshold — while AWS points at the Data Act as a shield against the DMA. But regulators are removing the *cheap* part of the exit. The UK CMA provisionally cleared committed-spend discounts while condemning egress fees, the reverse of how practitioners rank the two. The only first-person migration log in the corpus puts the real bill elsewhere: *"adapters were 1 day and the project was 30,"* with ~29 of 30 days spent re-tuning prompts and rebuilding evals. The most defensible framing isn't portability but *priced dependency* — "informed switching cost and negotiating control, not theoretical ability to move overnight." The arithmetic is wrecked upstream anyway: *23% average underestimation of cloud spend after migration completes*. And leaving buys you no protection from price risk — OVH warned of *87% hikes* this month.
