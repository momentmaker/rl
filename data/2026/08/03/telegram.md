🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-08-03*

Three numbers that everyone quotes and almost nobody defines. Today is about denominators.

*1. What a quoted hallucination rate actually measures*
The viral *86%* for GPT-5.5 is not "wrong 86% of the time." It is Artificial Analysis's AA-Omniscience rate: incorrect ÷ (incorrect + partial + not-attempted) — the denominator contains *only the questions it did not get right*. The clearest debunk in the window is a reply with six likes: 98 correct, 1 wrong, 1 "I don't know" scores *50%*; 99 correct and 1 wrong scores *100%*. Same model, near-perfect accuracy, catastrophic headline. GPT-5.5 also holds the highest accuracy ever recorded at *57%* — the two numbers are one fact said twice: it knows more, and it almost never abstains. Cross-leaderboard comparison is meaningless: Llama 4 Maverick is *4.6%* on Vectara and *87.6%* on AA-Omniscience — one scores summarization faithfulness, the other closed-book recall. Even Vectara is two datasets and most citations use the *retired* one. The benchmark's own peer review says the denominator is wrong — i/(p+i+a) lets a model improve by *abstaining more without learning anything*. And the one real movement this month runs the direction the metric punishes: Opus 5 gained *+7* accuracy over 4.8 while hallucination rose *+14* to 50%. The substitute for a leaderboard rank: your own harness plus an abstention-rate gate.

*2. What breaks in full-duplex voice agents*
The advice numbers are in milliseconds and the measured medians are in seconds. Every guide converges near-verbatim on a *200-400ms* turn gap, *<2%* false barge-in and *<60ms* TTS flush — while Cekura's benchmark puts the fastest platform, ElevenLabs, at a *1.73s median*. The honest record isn't blogs, it's the LiveKit issue tracker (12K stars, *733 open issues*): default end_of_turn_delay lands at *700-800ms*, and lowering it makes the agent cut users off. Worse, interruption logic is shared across thinking and speaking states, so "hard to interrupt but friendly to slow speakers" is currently *unbuildable*. Barge-in is a systems number — a round trip you cannot read off LLM traces — and the named failure is that STT, LLM and TTS each work correctly while the interruption still fails, because "the glue is the product, and the glue was bolted on last." The in-window architectural move is deleting VAD from the turn decision entirely in favour of STT-side or semantic end-of-turn. Full duplex shipped as a *product* (GPT-Live) with no API, so everyone still hand-builds it on a fast half-duplex Realtime API. New target metric: false-barge-in rate, tested against coughs, keyboards and a second voice in the room — barge-in happens in ~1 in 5 calls.

*3. What actually happens when you assign a real ticket to a coding agent*
The button went GA in all three trackers this month — Linear on 23 July, Jira five days earlier — and *not one vendor published a merge rate*. The only public dataset is Microsoft's own: *878 PRs in dotnet/runtime, 535 merged, 67.9%*, and 68.6% across seven repos. Treat a third of assigned tickets as rework you still have to read. Success is a property of the *ticket*: cleanup merges at *84.7%*, performance work at *54.5%* — the agent "can implement a complicated SIMD optimization if you tell it exactly what to do, but struggles with the analysis part." The highest-leverage variable isn't the model at all: *38.1% → 69%* from fixing setup instructions and a 20-minute build timeout, "not through better AI models, but through better preparation." The bottleneck moved to review and review got *worse* — a CMU synthesis of 3,100 documents finds agent PRs draw lower review rates, faster merges and less discussion. The biggest on-topic story of the month wasn't a benchmark but a breach (*541 points*): the issue body is untrusted input to an authenticated agent with repo access. And the tracker button is a spend button — subagents inherit the parent's frontier model with no way to route cheap work down.
