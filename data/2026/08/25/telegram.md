🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-08-25*

Three things built for one purpose and quietly repurposed — the repurposing is the story.

*1. What a crowdsourced WiFi map became*
WiGLE holds *1.73B* WiFi networks and ~*23B* location observations, logged by volunteers since 2001. This month almost none of the energy is about WiFi. The pipeline now runs *wardrive → WiGLE → Flock Finder*, mapping Flock Safety's licence-plate cameras. A hobby built on logging other people's routers became infrastructure for logging the people doing the logging. The sharpest item is the vendor counter-adapting in public, on *9 August* at 397 likes: "Flock are changing their hardware ids to prevent from being detected... We can expect a rotating set of hwids from them." Detection matches *OUI prefixes* — the manufacturer half of a MAC — against a community list holding *31 active Flock prefixes*. Rotating IDs beats it, because passive detection only sees what is already listed. And the famous opt-out is weaker than its reputation — Google and Apple honour `_nomap`, Microsoft wants `_optout`, and the scheme is *voluntary courtesy* from 2011, not a property right.

*2. Where independent search engines actually get an index*
The set that *owns a crawler* is far smaller than the set marketing itself as one. Brave and Mojeek own indexes; DuckDuckGo, Startpage and Qwant are privacy plays on someone else's results; SearXNG *doesn't crawl at all*. Kagi is openly hybrid — Google, Bing, Mojeek, Brave, *Marginalia* — so Marginalia's index is a supplier to a commercial product. Scale spans two orders of magnitude: Marginalia at ~*300M documents* on an *8-to-10-week* refresh with no Google or Bing feed, Mojeek at *9B pages*, Brave at *30B*. (That first figure is a self-described outdated 2024 FAQ.) The economics inverted in twelve months. Microsoft *retired the Bing Search APIs on 11 August 2025*, deleting the cheap tier that let anyone add search without crawling. Then agent demand arrived with money: Brave shipped an *LLM Context API*, Parallel raised *$100M* for its own index. Crawling went from a principled loss to venture-backed infrastructure. The legal route delivers nothing yet — Mehta's remedy orders Google to share its index with Qualified Competitors, but it is under appeal into *late 2026 or early 2027*. What people actually use these engines for is not replacing Google: "Somewhere out there is a website some guy built in 2004 about the history of lighthouse lenses... you will never ever find it on Google."

*3. AI NPCs, and what gamers are actually angry about*
NVIDIA shipped the *ACE Game Agent SDK* beta at Unreal Fest 2026 — open source, with Unreal Engine 5 plugins for speech recognition, a small language model and TTS. The number that matters is the floor: *entirely local on RTX*, no cloud, *8GB of VRAM*, an RTX 3060 works. That kills the latency and per-token cost objections in one move. Every shipped implementation uses a *small* model — PUBG Ally runs Mistral-Nemo-Minitron-8B locally. The sharpest verdict came from a shipped title pruning itself: inZOI's July notes list "Smart Zoi improvements coming" beside "AI building feature discontinued" — it kept the generative *character* and cut the generative *asset tool*. Meanwhile one person's Skyrim companion hit *367 points* on HN, outdrawing every corporate announcement in the window. But the loudest gaming conversation is not about NPCs at all: *5,028 upvotes* for a lead writer reportedly replaced by ChatGPT mid-development, "All the passenger voices were AI too... they're not disclosing it on Steam." The demand in every angry thread is *disclosure*, and the target is undisclosed labour replacement — a different thing from a companion that helps you loot. The regulatory catch is neat: twelve US states now regulate AI companions, and a Harvard analysis argues those definitions arguably capture NPCs that remember your choices across dozens of hours. The memory is the product feature and the legal exposure at once.
