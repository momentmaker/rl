🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-08-18*

Three arguments where the number everyone quotes isn't the number that matters.

*1. What data centers actually do to your electricity bill*
The strongest data runs *opposite* to the complaint: retail prices *decreased 3.5% for every doubling of data center capacity* from 2015–2024 (Fortune), with LBNL and EPRI data pointing the same way. Every one of those numbers ends in 2024 or 2025 — the anger is about 2026. The variable that actually decides your bill isn't demand, it's *the tariff*: whether a 25MW+ customer must "pay for most of the capacity you reserve even if you use less" on an 8-year-plus commitment, or whether default rules spread that infrastructure cost across every household. Virginia flipped that switch this month (1,137 points on r/Virginia). Meanwhile the politics have detached from the economics: the three biggest threads of the window are a teacher *arrested for clapping* at an anti-data-center hearing (37K points), residents bringing a *guillotine* to one (27K), and the arrest count itself (23K). NY passed a permit moratorium; a 1¢/kWh excise tax is on the table. The grid operator's own fix is neither — make 50MW+ loads *first to be cut* in a shortage and bring their own generation. And the quiet Bloomberg line under it all: *most power sought for US data centers will never materialize*, so the real ratepayer risk may be funding wires to buildings that never open. Nobody has an audited national figure; the viral "$23 billion added to bills" traces to an unsourced post.

*2. What actually keeps an archive alive*
Nine PBS nearly lost *50TB covering 70 years* of regional TV — and nothing decayed. Its storage vendor simply went out of business; the drives sat intact in an Iron Mountain facility while the station had no legal or physical way to reach them. A judge cleared it to retrieve them *today*. The lesson the field states plainly and hobbyists blur: *"a backup is a short-term data recovery solution... fundamentally different"* from preservation (Florida Virtual Campus), because durability "comes from constant stewardship... continuously recreated through copying, verification" (Gizmodo). An archive is a *verb*. Meanwhile the month's biggest threads measured media endurance beautifully and irrelevantly — 3 years of microSD testing (4.2K points), 8TB drives at 10 years (5.6K), a peer-reviewed study of *443,000* Backblaze drives — the exact variable that did *not* fail here. The thread that matters is "3-2-1 backup: where is your *1*?", 241 comments of people realizing their offsite copy is one account at one company. And a loss mode no storage strategy touches — automated moderation erased *a decade* of r/AskHistorians work.

*3. What archives and small sites do about AI crawlers*
Bots are now *57.5% of all web traffic*, and your dashboard shows none of it — analytics run on JavaScript, and crawlers never fire it. One operator pulled raw server logs: GPTBot 400+/day, Meta-ExternalAgent 220, ClaudeBot 180, PerplexityBot 90. *890 AI visits a day; Google Analytics reported zero.* The institutions describing real outages aren't startups, they're public collections — two Drupal conference talks this month on bots "scraping public government websites," which "hit fast, often ignore robots.txt," concluding *"yesterday's defenses are no longer enough."* The stack that consolidated: *Anubis* (21K stars), a proof-of-work toll that "weighs the soul of incoming HTTP requests," plus the *ai.robots.txt* blocklist (4.1K). But blocking is one of three live answers — TIME now serves *bots a different website, with ads built in*. The split that complicates blanket blocking: ~85% of AI requests are broad training crawls, ~15% are fetches from a real user's question. And the argument with the most energy isn't bandwidth — it's that *the web's collective memory is disappearing* (937 points) as the crawl-to-referral bargain breaks.
