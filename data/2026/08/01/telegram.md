🎲 *Random Learning — 2026-08-01*

Three rabbit holes, one shape: the loudest numbers in each field are the ones nobody independently published.

*1. AI-found vulnerabilities: what got verified versus what got claimed*
The one hard before-and-after came from a vendor grading its own codebase — Google fixed *1,072 security bugs* across two Chrome releases, more than the prior 23 milestones combined, including a sandbox escape that had been sitting there *13 years*. Point the same agents at someone else's project and the numbers turn into claims: one firm advertises *250+ CVEs* in OpenSSL, curl and the kernel, while the only independent verified-credit tracker holds *five* records total. Nobody published a reconciliation. The 432-CVE kernel dump everyone reached for isn't the story either — an audit found bulk assignment to already-fixed bugs, and only *three* disclosures even say "static analysis." Maintainers stopped arguing and closed the door: curl took *no security reports at all* for a month, libexpat joined, and the driver is precision not volume — confirmed vulnerabilities fell from *15% to under 5%*. The split isn't pro- vs anti-AI; the same Torvalds who called the security list "almost entirely unmanageable" also refused to let the kernel be an anti-AI project. The settlement forming: confirm exploitability and bring a patch, or don't file. One popular "1,596 disclosed, only 97 fixed" stat is single-source with 2 likes — don't repeat it.

*2. How AI products get priced once inference is the cost of goods sold*
Per-seat did not die. Across *65 software companies, exactly 4* have fully adopted outcome-based pricing; *72%* run a hybrid that staples AI credits onto a seat they still charge for — and, as the analysts put it, they "continue to generate revenue regardless of whether customers achieve desired outcomes." Where outcome pricing is real it's two categories, support and sales, in a band of *$0.99 to ~$2.00 per resolution* — and the design tell is resolution vs conversation, since one competitor bills per conversation regardless. The margin answer is now specific: *~52%* gross margin against SaaS's 70-80%, because inference posts straight into COGS, with *25-30% of revenue* as the pressure line. Note who's saying it — every margin number comes from investors and benchmarks, *never from the companies charging per resolution*. The sharpest argument is a negative: Microsoft spent one month bundling Copilot into a 2x-priced tier and moving two products to metered billing, and never shipped outcome pricing, despite having more usage data than almost anyone. And the honest definition came from a practitioner: nobody measures outcomes, they charge at "the closest auditable proxy to value" — re-chosen every 3-6 months.

*3. Whether text-to-CAD actually produces parametric models you can edit*
The most useful finding is an absence. A sweep of *eight* CAD and 3D-printing subreddits returned 22 threads and *54,264 upvotes* — and not one about AI-generated CAD. On HN, the single on-topic submission all month finished at *6 points, zero comments*. So every confident claim traces to a vendor blog, a benchmark paper or a README — not a maker holding a printed part. What vendors do admit is useful: the *parametric gap* is the failure, and the test is one keystroke — add a chamfer or change a dimension, and mesh output falls apart while parametric output doesn't. That's the whole case for LLM-written CadQuery or OpenSCAD: the parameters are literally variables in a file you own. The failures there are *spatial, not syntactic* — floating walls, clipped floors, rotations the wrong way — so the fix is handing coordinate-frame algebra to deterministic code. The real 30-day movement is verification loops shipping as code: generate, *execute, measure the geometry, loop until it verifies*. And the structurally different bet is skipping generation entirely — drive FreeCAD's kernel over MCP and let the kernel enforce geometry instead of asking a model to emit it.
